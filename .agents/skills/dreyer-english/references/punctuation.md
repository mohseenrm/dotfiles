# Punctuation: 67 Things To Do (and Not Do)

Condensed from *Dreyer's English* ch. 3. Grouped by mark. Each rule keeps its original number.

## PERIODS

**1.** One space after a sentence-ending period, not two. Search-and-replace to remove stray double spaces.

**2.** Drop the periods in acronyms and initialisms: FBI not F.B.I., UNESCO not U.N.E.S.C.O. Academic degrees may go either way (PhD or Ph.D.), but favor the periodless forms for longer degrees.

**3.** Two-letter postal state abbreviations (MA, NY, CA) take no periods and belong only on envelopes. Elsewhere use the traditional forms (Mass., N.Y., Calif.) or spell the state out.

**4.** Keep the periods in U.S., and prefer using it only as an adjective ("U.S. foreign policy"); as a noun, write out "the United States."

**5.** A statement shaped like a question but not really asking one can end with a period instead of a question mark. It makes a point, doesn't it.

## COMMAS

**6.** Always use the series (Oxford/serial) comma: the comma before the final "and"/"or" in a list, as in "apples, pears, and cherries." It prevents the last two items from reading as a pair. Note that a comma alone cannot fix every ambiguous list; some sentences need rewriting, not repunctuating.

**7.** Don't rely on "use it only when needed for clarity." People misjudge where clarity requires it, and applying it every time is simpler and frees attention for real problems.

**8.** Exception: when a series uses an ampersand instead of "and" (mostly in titles and firm names), skip the series comma, as in "Eats, Shoots & Leaves."

**9.** A short, clear introductory phrase needs no comma ("On Friday she went to school"). The longer the introductory element, the more you'll want a comma after it.

**10.** Set off an introductory element with a comma when omitting it crashes words together confusingly, e.g. "In June, Truman's secretary of state flew to Moscow" (so "June" doesn't read as a name).

**11.** Don't add a comma where it undercuts the meaning: "Suddenly he ran from the room" needs no comma, since one after "Suddenly" makes it less sudden.

**12.** Avoid comma splices (joining two independent sentences with only a comma). Exceptions: short, closely linked clauses ("He came, he saw, he conquered") and stylistic fiction. A long sentence is only a "run-on" when it is punctuated wrongly, not merely because it is long.

**13.** Use the vocative comma to set off a name in direct address: "I'll meet you in the bar, Charlie," and also "And, Dad, here's another thing." Capitalize honorifics used in place of names ("Your Majesty," "Mom" in address). Keep "my Aunt Phyllis" when it's how she's called, but "my grandmother Maude" when naming which one.

**14.** Precede or follow dialogue with a comma ("Atticus said, 'Do not...'"), but not when the quotation follows a form of "to be": "Lloyd's last words were 'That tiger looks highly pettable.'"

**15.** Use a comma before a sentence-ending "too" only if you hear a pause there; otherwise leave it out. Either choice is defensible.

**16.** Use the "only" comma (restrictive vs. nonrestrictive) to set off a noun that is the only one of its kind: "Lincoln's eldest son, Robert, was born..." (inessential name, commas) versus "Lincoln's son Robert" (which son, no commas). Don't set the comma where there's no uniqueness ("The Pulitzer Prize-winning novelist Edith Wharton," no commas).

## Best Illustration of the Necessity of the "Only" Comma I've Ever Managed to Rustle Up

**17.** Apply the same logic to "that" vs. "which": essential info takes "that" and no comma ("the Bible that's on the table"); inessential info takes "which" and a comma ("the Bible, which is on the table").

**18.** A comma that opens an interrupting clause must be matched by a comma that closes it: "Queen Victoria, who ruled..., was the longest-reigning monarch." Watch the closing comma, which often goes missing, especially when a parenthetical is nested inside the interruption.

## COLONS

**19.** If a full sentence follows a colon, capitalize its first word; if a list or fragment follows, keep it lowercase.

## APOSTROPHES

**20.** Never use an apostrophe to pluralize a word: bananas, potatoes, Trumans, Obamas, not "banana's" or "Truman's."

**21.** Pluralize abbreviations without apostrophes: CDs, IDs, ATMs.

**22.** Likewise no apostrophe in dos and don'ts, yeses and nos.

**23.** "Their's" and "your's" are not words.

**24.** Do use an apostrophe to pluralize a single letter: mind your p's and q's, dot your i's, four B's and two C's.

**25.** For possessives, add 's even to singular nouns ending in s: "the boss's office," "Charles Dickens's novels," "Jane Jacobs's advocacy." Apply this uniformly rather than varying by pronunciation, including "Jesus's" and "Xerxes's."

**26.** Watch for the easy typo of misplacing the apostrophe: "Jane Jacobs's activism," not "Jane Jacob's activism."

**27.** For a name with a comma-set "Jr.," possessivize as "Donald Trump, Jr.'s, perfidy" (both commas) or drop the commas entirely ("Donald Trump Jr.'s perfidy"). Never write "period-comma-apostrophe" forms like "JR.,'S."

**28.** Form plural family names before possessivizing: the Trumans, the Kennedys, the Obamas, the Adamses, the Hayeses. Then the possessive: "the Trumans' daughter," "the Adamses' correspondence."

**29.** Separate ownership takes each name possessive ("Jeanette's and Nelson's pencils"); joint ownership takes only the last name possessive ("Jeanette and Nelson's pencils").

**30.** Prefer "farmers market" (a market made up of farmers), with no apostrophe, over "farmer's" or "farmers'."

**31.** Don't elide a title's "The" in a possessive; "Carson McCullers's The Heart Is a Lonely Hunter" reads better than dropping the article.

## SEMICOLONS

**32.** Use semicolons to separate list items that themselves contain commas: "pizza; fried pork dumplings; Venice, Italy; and Puccini's operas." They also link closely related independent clauses to hold tightly woven ideas together rather than breaking them into separate sentences.

## PARENTHESES

**33.** A midsentence parenthetical starts lowercase and takes no terminal punctuation inside (unless it's a question or exclamation). At a sentence end, keep the period outside the closing parenthesis. A freestanding parenthetical sentence starts capitalized with its terminal punctuation inside.

**34.** A parenthetical (or comma- or dash-set) aside doesn't make a plural subject singular: "why I care what this nonentity (and her eerie husband) think," not "thinks." But "to say nothing of," "as well as," or "not to mention" does make the subject singular.

**35.** Don't overuse parentheses, especially for coy asides; too many makes your writing preening.

**36.** Beware that editors trimming for length tend to target parentheticals first for deletion.

## BRACKETS

**37.** Use brackets for a parenthetical inside a parenthetical (but avoid the ugliness), and to enclose any text you insert into or alter within a quotation. Exception: you may silently change an initial capital to lowercase or vice versa, except in legal or contentious scholarly work, where even that gets bracketed ("[a]ll government is cruel").

**39.** Use [sic] (italic, bracketed) to flag that an error in quoted material is the original author's, not yours. For lots of archaic material, note the verbatim quoting up front instead of littering [sic]. Never use [sic] to sneer at the sense of what someone wrote, and don't slap it on legitimate British/American spelling differences ("labour").

## QUOTATION MARKS

**40.** Put small works in roman type with quotation marks (songs, poems, short stories, TV episodes); put large works in italics (albums, poetry volumes, books, TV series). E.g. "The Lottery" but *The Lottery and Other Stories*.

**41.** Set named paintings and sculptures in italics (*The Luncheon on the Grass*); set unofficial titles in roman without quotes (the Victory of Samothrace).

**42.** Set off dialogue with quotation marks; omit them only if you're skilled enough to keep narration and dialogue clearly distinct.

**43.** Render interior thought without quotation marks and without italics: "What is to become of me? Estelle thought."

**44.** Don't use quotation marks for emphasis (use italics). Avoid scare quotes too; they read as snotty and quickly date.

**45.** Don't put quotation marks after "so-called"; it's redundant. Quotes after "known as" are fine when introducing an unfamiliar term.

**46.** To refer to a word as a word, either quotation marks or italics work: "the fact that" or *the fact that*. Quotes read chattier, italics more technical; choose by taste.

**47.** A question mark or exclamation point goes outside the closing quotation marks when it belongs to the whole sentence, not the quote. When both quote and sentence want emphatic punctuation, pick one or rewrite; don't stack "!"! or "?"?.

**48.** In American English, use double quotation marks first, single for a quote within a quote, double again for a third level. Avoid deep nesting, and watch that you don't accidentally set doubles inside doubles.

**49.** Periods and commas always go inside closing quotation marks; semicolons go outside.

## HYPHENS

**50.** Hyphenation of compounds is inconsistent (e.g. "light-headed" but "lighthearted"), so when it matters, just look the compound up in the dictionary.

**51.** Compounds tend to lose their hyphens and close up over time ("light bulb" to "lightbulb," "Web site" to "website"). Writers drive this change, so use the closed form if you want it to become standard.

**52.** Hyphenate multiword modifiers before a noun ("first-rate movie," "man-eating shark") to prevent misreading. Convention allows exceptions where misreading is unlikely ("real estate agent," "high school students"). Do not hyphenate an "-ly" adverb plus adjective ("inconsistently applied rules," "highly paid editors").

**53.** Close up prefix + word without a hyphen (antiwar, codependent, nonnative, preexisting, reelect, subpar). Keep a hyphen only when the closed form is genuinely unreadable or hideous, and choose those battles rarely.

**54.** Hyphenate a prefix to distinguish meaning: re-create (create anew) vs. recreate (enjoy recreation); re-form (form again) vs. reform; re-sign (sign again) vs. resign.

**55.** Suffixes attach without a hyphen (-ing, -ism, -less, -ness). If a suffixed form looks bad ("rubelike"), reword rather than hyphenating.

**56.** Get children's-age constructions right: "six years old," "my six-year-old daughter," "a six-year-old." Not "a six-year old girl."

**57.** Close up compound vulgarities rather than hyphenating them; hyphenated vulgarities look daintily comic.

## DASHES

**59.** Em dashes (the long dash) set off interruptions and parenthetical asides where commas won't do; use no more than two per sentence. En dashes (shorter, longer than a hyphen) join multiword compounds ("a Meryl Streep-Robert De Niro comedy," "a World War II-era plane," "a Pulitzer Prize-winning play") and connect ranges: page references (pp. 3-21), scores (14-2), and votes (7-2). Don't overload en dashes; rephrase when the meaning gets strained.

## QUESTION MARKS AND EXCLAMATION POINTS

**60.** In casual prose or dialogue, a question-shaped statement not meant as a question can end with a period: "That's a horrible idea, isn't it."

**61.** Go light on exclamation points; overused, they grow bossy and wearying.

**62.** Still, use an exclamation point where genuine urgency or warmth needs it ("Your hair is on fire!"), since a period can read as sarcastic or flat.

**63.** Don't end sentences with double exclamation points or double question marks (outside comic books).

**64.** Don't use "?!" or "!?".

**65.** Don't use the interrobang.

**66.** Sentences beginning "I wonder" are declarations, not questions, and end with a period: "I wonder who's kissing her now."

**67.** Sentences beginning "Guess who" or "Guess what" are imperatives, not questions, and end with a period: "Guess who's coming to dinner."
