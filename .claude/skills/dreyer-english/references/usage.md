# Usage, Numbers & Grammar Heuristics

Hand-curated from *Dreyer's English* chs. 1, 4, 6 (plus a few peeves). These are defaults, not commandments: respect the writer's voice and register.

## Wan Intensifiers & Throat Clearers (ch. 1)

Words that usually add nothing. Write freely, then delete these on the pass-back. If the sentence feels weaker without one, find a stronger word instead of propping it up.

The core twelve to hunt: **very, rather, really, quite, in fact, just** (as in "merely"), **so** (as in "extremely"), **pretty** (as in "pretty tedious"), **of course, surely, that said, actually.**

Related deletables and hedges to watch: needless, in order to (usually just "to"), the fact that, it is important to note, at the end of the day, basically, essentially.

## Trim principle (ch. 12, summarized)

"Two Words Where One Will Do." After a draft, scan for redundancy and restatement. See `trimmables.md` for the redundancy list. Prose, like anyone, can stand to lose a little weight; your prose is the easier target.

## Numbers (ch. 4)

- **Spell out one through one hundred**, and round/simple numbers beyond that expressible in a couple of words (two hundred, eighteen hundred). Use numerals for the rest: 250, 1,823. (Space-tight periodicals cut the write-out limit to nine or ten; if you have room, words look friendlier.)
- **Consistency within a comparison wins.** If one number in a related set needs numerals, put them all in numerals: "12 cows, 37 mules, and 126 chickens" (unrelated counts, like acreage, can stay in words).
- **Avoid numerals in dialogue** ("I bought sixteen apples"), but don't spell out to absurdity: write "4:32" and "Route 66," not "four thirty-two" and "Route Sixty-six," when numerals read more naturally.
- Number-heavy subjects (finance, science, stats) use numerals throughout by default.
- If a rule-following choice looks awful or confusing on the page, rethink it.

## Grammar heuristics (ch. 6)

- **who / whom.** "who" is the cousin of I/he/she/they (the subject, doing the thing); "whom" is the cousin of me/him/her/them (the object, done to). Beware hypercorrection: don't reach for "whom" where "who" is right ("whomever wanted it" is wrong; the relative pronoun is the subject of the following verb, not the object of the preceding preposition). Often you can just drop it: "The man Shirley met for lunch."
- **that / which.** Restrictive clause (essential, no comma) takes "that": "the car that won." Nonrestrictive clause (extra info, set off by commas) takes "which": "the car, which was red, won."
- **not only X but (also) Y / either X or Y.** Keep the two halves parallel: what follows "only" must grammatically match what follows "but." "not only about X but about Y," not "not only about X but Y."
- **Split infinitives are fine.** "To boldly go" is not an error. Split when it reads better; don't contort to avoid it.
- **Ending a sentence with a preposition is fine.** Rephrase only when the result is genuinely cleaner, not out of superstition.
- **none** can take a singular or plural verb; both are correct. Don't force "none is."
- **Dangling modifiers** are a real error: the introductory phrase must attach to the sentence's subject. "Walking to the store, the rain started" (the rain wasn't walking) - recast it.

## A few peeves worth honoring (chs. 2, 9)

- **its / it's.** "its" = possessive; "it's" = "it is" / "it has." No apostrophe in the possessive.
- **fewer / less.** "fewer" for countable things (fewer cars), "less" for mass/uncountable (less traffic).
- **comprise.** The whole comprises the parts: "the album comprises ten songs." Not "is comprised of" (widely disliked).
- **begs the question** properly means assumes-the-conclusion, not raises-the-question; many readers wince at the loose use.
- **literally** means literally. Don't use it for emphasis on the figurative.
- **decimate** originally meant reduce by a tenth; using it for "devastate" is common but noticed.
